Toy ECC (secp256k1) vs Caesar cipher

Encryption, decryption and two attacks: statistical (Caesar) and key-space search (toy ECC). Everything runs in your browser.

1 · Toy ECC encryption on secp256k1

The curve is the real secp256k1 (y² = x³ + 7 over a 256-bit prime), but the private key is restricted to a small range. The scheme is ElGamal/ECIES-style: the receiver’s public key is Q = kG; the sender picks a random r, sends C1 = rG and encrypts the message with a keystream derived from the shared point S = rQ = kC1.


Click the button.
Steps: (1) k random in the small range, Q = kG. (2) Sender picks random 256-bit r, C1 = rG, S = rQ. (3) Keystream = SHA-256(S.x ‖ counter); ciphertext = message XOR keystream. (4) Receiver computes S = kC1 and XORs again. Without k (or r), S cannot be computed; k is the secret, Q and C1 are public.

3 · Statistical comparison of the two ciphertexts

Both schemes encrypt the same ~700-letter English text; we measure Shannon entropy per byte and the share of the most frequent byte.

Similarities and differences

  • Procedure. Both are symmetric in the last step: Caesar adds the key to each letter and subtracts it to decrypt; the ECC scheme XORs a keystream and XORs it again. The ECC scheme is a public-key scheme: the sender needs only Q, and only the holder of k can compute S. Caesar is purely symmetric: both sides share the same secret.
  • Key space. Caesar has 26 keys (≈4.7 bits). The toy ECC scheme has 2b keys for a b-bit restricted key (here 28–220); proper secp256k1 has about 2256 keys.
  • Statistics. Caesar is a fixed substitution: the letter histogram is only shifted, so entropy equals the plaintext’s (≈4 bits) and the most frequent letter stays about as frequent. The ECC ciphertext is XORed with a hash-derived keystream, so bytes look near uniform (entropy close to 8 bits, limited only by the sample size). Statistics reveal nothing about k.

6 · Discussion

  • Different attacks, different information. The Caesar attack uses statistics of the language: the ciphertext leaks the letter distribution, so we can score candidate keys without any known plaintext. Short texts fail (see the table) because the sample is too small. The ECC attack uses no statistics at all: the ciphertext looks random, and we only exploit that k lives in a small set, checking each candidate against the public key Q. It succeeds with certainty, independent of message length.
  • Why it does not scale. Brute force costs about 2b point additions; baby-step giant-step (or Pollard’s rho) about 2b/2 with memory for BSGS, rho needs almost none. For the full secp256k1 group (order ≈ 2256) that is ≈ 2128 operations, far beyond any computer. No known algorithm solves the ECDLP on a well-chosen curve much faster than this generic square-root bound.
  • Weakness from the restricted key space: exactly the two attacks above. A uniformly random k in the full range removes them.
  • Weaknesses from the toy design itself (even with a large key): no authentication (a MAC or AEAD is missing), so ciphertexts are malleable: flipping a ciphertext bit flips the same plaintext bit. The keystream is a home-made KDF of S.x only (a standard KDF/AEAD should be used). If r is ever reused or generated badly, two messages share a keystream and XORing them leaks the plaintexts; known-plaintext reveals the keystream. The message length is not hidden. There is also no protection against invalid-curve points sent as C1 (C1 should be validated).
  • Caesar’s own weaknesses come from the design, not just the tiny key space: even with a larger alphabet, a fixed substitution preserves frequencies and can be attacked statistically.
